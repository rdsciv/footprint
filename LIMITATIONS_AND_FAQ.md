# LIMITATIONS & FAQ

Plain-language answers to the objections this methodology expects from energy researchers, sustainability practitioners, and a skeptical Hacker News audience. Read alongside `METHODOLOGY.md` for full derivations and citations.

---

## "Your numbers could be 10x off. Why show them at all?"

They could be, in the worst case — the compounded water/carbon bands in METHODOLOGY.md §5.1 can genuinely span close to an order of magnitude when hardware, batch occupancy, and grid-mix uncertainty stack unfavorably. We show them anyway, for the same reason a car's fuel gauge is worth having even though it isn't a certified fuel-flow meter: **directional, real-time feedback changes behavior in ways a precise-but-delayed annual report cannot.** A developer deciding whether to re-run a 50,000-token context window three more times to debug something, or whether a quick Haiku-class query would do, benefits from seeing "this is roughly 10x more expensive" even if neither number is precise to two decimal places. The alternative — showing nothing until someone can show something perfectly — guarantees the number stays invisible forever, since perfect measurement from outside a closed provider is not achievable (see "closed-model opacity" below). The design response to the uncertainty is not to hide the estimate; it's to never let the estimate imply more precision than it has (§5.2: 2 significant figures, explicit ranges on expand, explicit MEASURED/MODELED/SPECULATIVE labeling in the underlying data).

## "Marginal vs. average emissions — which one are you showing, and why does it matter?"

**Average** carbon intensity describes the emissions per kWh across *all* generation serving a grid over some period. **Marginal** carbon intensity describes the emissions of the *next increment* of generation that would be dispatched to meet one additional unit of demand right now — often a fast-responding gas peaker, which can be considerably dirtier than the grid's average mix. For a workload like an LLM query, whose consumption decision genuinely does add a small increment of demand at a specific moment, marginal intensity is arguably the more causally correct number to attribute to "what did this query actually cause." Average intensity is what's more commonly disclosed and easier to source (it's what's baked into a facility's location-based factor, METHODOLOGY.md §1.4), and it's what this methodology defaults to today for the coefficient table, precisely because marginal-intensity data requires a live, hourly, LMP-aware feed that a static coefficient table cannot provide. The live-signal roadmap (WattTime and similar services explicitly compute and expose marginal emissions signals) is the intended path to closing this gap — see `LIVE_SIGNAL_ROADMAP.md`. Until then: **the carbon number in this tool, if shown, is an average-intensity estimate, and should say so.**

## "Withdrawal vs. consumption — why should I care?"

Water **withdrawal** is water taken from a source (river, aquifer, municipal supply); water **consumption** is withdrawal minus whatever is returned to the source. A once-through cooling system can withdraw a lot of water and return nearly all of it (warmed) to the same river — high withdrawal, low consumption, and a very different local-water-stress story than a facility that evaporates most of what it withdraws. Google's own disclosure explicitly adopts the consumption framing because it "best represents impact on local water availability... the volume of water that is evaporated and therefore unavailable for reuse" (METHODOLOGY.md §4.2), and this methodology follows that convention: **all water figures in this tool are consumption-based unless explicitly labeled otherwise.** A tool that quietly reports withdrawal as if it were consumption (or vice versa) would materially misrepresent local water-stress impact, which is precisely the kind of quiet unit-conflation this methodology is built to avoid.

## "Closed-model opacity — how can you estimate anything for GPT-5 or Claude Opus if OpenAI and Anthropic don't publish parameter counts or hardware?"

We can't observe it directly, and we say so rather than pretending otherwise. The tier assignment for undisclosed-parameter flagship models in this methodology follows the same proxy Jegham et al. use in their peer-reviewed benchmarking study: classify by *inferred* performance characteristics (throughput, latency, reasoning capability track record) into the same hardware-class buckets used for models whose sizes *are* disclosed, rather than guessing a specific parameter count. This is explicitly a **proxy classification, not a measurement** — it is the best available substitute for information that is, by the provider's own choice, not public, and the resulting "frontier" tier in `coefficients.json` carries the widest uncertainty multiplier of the three tiers (±3x) specifically because of this added inferential step. If a provider begins publishing its own energy-per-query disclosure (as Google and, to a lesser extent, Mistral now do), that model should immediately move from the inferred-tier bucket to a MEASURED, provider-specific coefficient — the coefficient table is designed to be overridden per-model the moment better data exists, not to be a permanent guess.

## "Isn't 'directional telemetry, not carbon accounting' just a hedge to avoid being held accountable for wrong numbers?"

It's a scope statement, and it cuts both ways: it means we don't get to claim audit-grade precision, but it also means the tool isn't trying to substitute for one. Carbon accounting (GHG Protocol Scope 1/2/3 reporting, the kind that goes into a corporate sustainability report or a regulatory filing) requires verified activity data, audited emission factors, and a defined organizational boundary — none of which a statusline running client-side against a black-box API can produce. Claiming otherwise would be the actual greenwashing risk here. What this tool *can* do honestly is show relative, order-of-magnitude signal in real time: "this request was bigger than that one," "frontier-tier models cost several times more per token than small-tier ones," "afternoon usage in a hot climate probably carries a higher water cost than overnight usage in a cool one." That is a materially different and much more modest claim than "your carbon footprint this month was X kg CO2e, verified" — and the entire design of this methodology (uncertainty bands everywhere, explicit MEASURED/MODELED/SPECULATIVE labels, no display precision beyond what the data supports) exists to keep that distinction visible to the user, not to launder a weak estimate as a strong one.

## "Why should cost-based routing tell me anything about environmental impact?"

Mostly, today, it shouldn't — and this methodology says so explicitly rather than implying a link that isn't there (METHODOLOGY.md §3). The causal chain from wholesale electricity price to which facility serves your request is real in structure (locational marginal prices do reflect grid conditions; hyperscalers do run demand-response and off-peak-discount programs that respond to grid signals) but weak and heavily attenuated by hedged power procurement, capital-cost-dominated pricing, and the fact that no public multi-provider gateway currently routes on a carbon or energy signal at all — only price, latency, and reliability. We include the analysis in the methodology because the brief specifically asked for an honest assessment of this coupling, and "the effect is real but currently too weak and unmeasurable-from-outside to build a coefficient on" is itself a defensible, citable finding — not a reason to fabricate a routing-based carbon adjustment that would not survive scrutiny.

## "What happens when a number in here turns out to be wrong, or a source updates its disclosure?"

The coefficient table is versioned (`coefficients.json` carries a `$last_updated` field and a schema version) specifically so it can be revised as sources update — Google's own energy-per-prompt figure fell by a documented 33x over roughly a year of efficiency work, so a static, never-updated coefficient table would become stale in exactly the way this methodology is trying to avoid. Implementers should treat this file as a living artifact tied to the sources in `METHODOLOGY.md`'s References section, not a permanent constant.

## "Does displaying this number encourage people to use AI less, or does it risk normalizing AI energy use as fine because the number looks small?"

Neither is the goal, and the methodology deliberately avoids framing that pushes either way (§7: "instrumentation, not advocacy"). A per-query number of a few hundred milliwatt-hours is genuinely small next to many everyday activities (Google's own comparison: a median prompt uses less energy than nine seconds of a television running), and reporting that honestly is not the same as claiming AI's aggregate footprint is negligible — aggregate impact is a function of both per-query cost and query volume, and this tool deliberately stays out of the volume/aggregate argument, which is a different (and legitimately contested) question from the one a per-request statusline is built to answer.
