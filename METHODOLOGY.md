# METHODOLOGY.md

## Statusline Environmental Telemetry for AI Coding Agents

**Version:** 0.3.0 (tracks `coefficients.json` `$schema_version` and the `modelfootprint` package version)
**Scope:** Real-time energy (Wh), water (mL), and optional carbon (gCO2e) estimation displayed alongside token counters in a terminal statusline for AI coding agents (Claude Code and similar CLI/IDE agents).

**Changes in 0.3.0:** explicit accounting-boundary declaration for the energy coefficients (§2.2); water equation corrected to apply WUE at the IT-energy boundary and EWIF at the facility boundary (§1.2); two-signal (dry-bulb gate × wet-bulb intensity) live water model (§4.1); carbon envelope now includes grid-variability bounds for the static fallback and a modeled ±25% band for live CI (§1.4, §5.1); uncertainty combination renamed to what it is — a scenario envelope, not a confidence interval (§5.1); unknown-model estimates carry a cross-tier envelope (§2.2).

---

## 0. What this is, and what it is not

This document specifies a methodology for estimating — not measuring — the energy, water, and carbon footprint of individual LLM inference calls made by a coding agent, for display in a live statusline next to the token counter every developer already watches.

**This is directional telemetry, not carbon accounting.** No user-facing tool without access to a provider's internal power telemetry, PDU logs, cooling-loop instrumentation, or real-time grid mix can produce audit-grade figures. What follows is instead a physically-grounded, citation-anchored *order-of-magnitude estimator*: it is built from the best public disclosures and peer-reviewed measurements available (Google's instrumented production disclosure, Mistral's third-party-audited LCA, an academic benchmarking study, and a first-principles FLOP/energy model from Epoch AI), it states explicit uncertainty bands rather than false-precision point values, and it is designed so that every number a user sees can be traced back to a labeled source and an explicit set of assumptions.

The goal is not to tell a developer "this prompt cost exactly 0.31 Wh." The goal is to tell them "a typical prompt like this one costs on the order of a few hundred milliwatt-hours — roughly the energy of a phone charge for ten seconds — and that order of magnitude is worth knowing as you work," while being fully honest that the true figure for *this specific request*, on *this specific provider's actual hardware*, could plausibly be off by a factor of 2–5x in either direction, and that carbon/water estimates carry additional multipliers on top of that.

Where a number is directly reported by a primary source, it is labeled **MEASURED**. Where it is derived by combining measured inputs through a model (e.g., FLOP-counting × hardware wattage × utilization assumption), it is labeled **MODELED**. Where no public data exists and the estimate is our own extrapolation or engineering judgment, it is labeled **SPECULATIVE**, and speculative values are given the widest uncertainty bands and are the first candidates for replacement as better data becomes available (see `LIVE_SIGNAL_ROADMAP.md`).

## 1. Core model

### 1.1 Energy

```
energy_IT_Wh       = tok_in · e_in + tok_cache · e_cache + tok_out · e_out
energy_facility_Wh = energy_IT_Wh × PUE
```

- `tok_in` — input (prompt) tokens not served from a cache hit
- `tok_cache` — input tokens served from a prompt/context cache (prefix cache, KV-cache reuse)
- `tok_out` — output (completion) tokens generated by the model, including any hidden reasoning/thinking tokens
- `e_in`, `e_cache`, `e_out` — per-token energy coefficients (Wh/token), tiered by model class (§2), at the **IT-equipment (node) boundary** (§2.2)
- `PUE` — Power Usage Effectiveness: the ratio of total facility energy draw to IT-equipment energy draw, capturing cooling, power distribution, and other data-center overhead not already inside the per-token coefficients

The headline energy figure displayed to the user is `energy_facility_Wh`; the IT-boundary intermediate is kept because the water equation needs both (§1.2).

### 1.2 Water

```
water_L = energy_IT_kWh × WUE_site(location, t) + energy_facility_kWh × EWIF_grid(location, t)
```

- `WUE_site(location, t)` — on-site Water Usage Effectiveness (L/kWh of **IT energy**): water consumed directly for evaporative/adiabatic cooling at the data center, a function of location and time (via wet-bulb temperature, humidity, and cooling architecture). The Microsoft fleet disclosures this term is anchored to define WUE as water divided by *IT-equipment* kWh (§4.3), so it multiplies `energy_IT_kWh` — applying it after PUE would overstate direct water by the PUE factor.
- `EWIF_grid(location, t)` — off-site Electricity Water Intensity Factor (L/kWh of **grid electricity**): water consumed upstream to generate the electricity the data center draws — all of it, cooling overhead included — so it multiplies `energy_facility_kWh`.
- This additive on-site + off-site structure follows Ren et al.'s decomposition of AI water footprint into scope-1 (on-site) and scope-2 (off-site, embedded in electricity) consumption (§4).

### 1.3 Carbon (optional)

```
carbon_g = energy_facility_kWh × CI_grid(location, t)
```

- `CI_grid(location, t)` — carbon intensity of the electricity grid serving the facility at time t (gCO2e/kWh), which can be reported on a **location-based** (physical grid mix) or **market-based** (adjusted for the operator's clean-energy procurement / PPAs / RECs) accounting basis — these can differ by 2–4x for the same facility (§1.4) and must never be silently conflated.

### 1.4 Location-based vs. market-based carbon — a mandatory distinction

Google's own environmental-impact disclosure makes this distinction explicit and uses it to compute two materially different carbon-intensity figures for the *same* electricity: Google's 2024 location-based emission factor was 345 gCO2e/kWh and the reduction associated with its procured clean energy was equivalent to 251 gCO2e/kWh, so the net market-based emission factor was 94 gCO2e/kWh [2]. For 2023, the location-based factor was 366 gCO2e/kWh with a 231 gCO2e/kWh procurement reduction, yielding a 135 gCO2e/kWh market-based factor [2].

This is a **3.7x spread** (94 vs. 345 gCO2e/kWh) between the two accounting bases for the same company's same electricity in the same year. A statusline that reports carbon must declare which basis it uses in its footer/help text, every time — silently picking one is a defensibility failure. Location-based is the more conservative default for a *directional* tool, because market-based figures depend on contractual REC/PPA claims a third party cannot verify from outside the company, and because location-based better reflects the physical reality of "what generation resources were dispatched to serve this load" — which is also the basis most compatible with hourly marginal-emissions APIs (WattTime, Electricity Maps) intended for live-signal integration (see roadmap).

## 2. Per-token energy coefficients (Task 1)

### 2.1 Primary sources

Four independent lines of evidence anchor the coefficient table, each with a different methodology and different blind spots:

1. **Epoch AI** (Josh You, Feb 2025) — a bottom-up FLOP-counting model, not a measurement. Epoch AI finds that typical ChatGPT queries using GPT-4o likely consume roughly 0.3 watt-hours [1], revising an earlier widely-cited estimate downward by roughly 10x. The method estimates total FLOPs per query from output token count and (estimated) active parameters, divides by H100 peak throughput, and applies a utilization discount; Epoch explicitly used 10% as a rough estimate of typical utilization rates for inference clusters [1] and accounted for server/data-center overhead by noting H100 clusters can consume up to ~1500 W per GPU due to the overhead costs of servers and data centers [1] versus a 700 W board rating. This is a **MODELED** figure with wide assumption-driven uncertainty (utilization and active-parameter count are both unverifiable estimates for closed models).

2. **Google's own instrumented production disclosure** (arXiv:2508.15734, Aug 2025) is the strongest **MEASURED** anchor available, because it comes from actual telemetry on Google's serving fleet rather than an outside reconstruction. The median Gemini Apps text prompt uses 0.24 Wh of energy, emits 0.03 gCO2e, and consumes 0.26 mL of water [2] under Google's full-stack accounting, which explicitly includes idle-capacity and host-CPU overhead that narrower methodologies omit — applying a non-comprehensive methodology that only considers active TPU and GPU consumption, the median Gemini text prompt is estimated at 0.10 Wh, 0.02 gCO2e, and 0.12 mL [2]. The ratio between these two numbers (0.24 / 0.10 ≈ 2.4x) is itself an important artifact: **it demonstrates that measurement boundary alone can move a headline number by 2–3x before any argument about hardware or workload begins.** Google states this full-stack scaling factor is **1.72x** over accelerator-only energy (vs. an assumed 2x in prior public estimates).

3. **Mistral's Life Cycle Assessment** (with ADEME + Carbone 4, mid-2025), the only figure in this set independently reviewed under a formal LCA methodology. Each 400-token prompt for Mistral Large 2, accounting for around 300 words, directly led to 1.14 gCO2e and the consumption of 45 mL of water [3]. This is a **MEASURED/audited** figure for one specific model class (a ~123B-parameter dense model) and cannot be assumed to generalize across architectures, but it is valuable as a cross-check: Jegham et al.'s independently-modeled estimate for the same model, normalized to 400 tokens, comes out within one standard deviation of Mistral's disclosed number (§2.2), which is meaningful convergent validation between a bottom-up model and a top-down audited disclosure.

4. **"How Hungry is AI?"** (Jegham, Abdelatti, Koh, Elmoubarki & Hendawi, arXiv:2505.09598) is the most directly useful **MODELED** source for a multi-tier coefficient table, because it benchmarks 30 commercial models rather than one. Results show the most energy-intensive models exceed 29 Wh per long prompt, over 65x the most efficient systems [4], and cross-validate against two independent disclosures: their short-query estimate lands within 19% of Sam Altman's publicly stated 0.34 Wh/query figure, and their Mistral Large 2 estimate (normalized) lands within one standard deviation of Mistral's own disclosed 1.14 gCO2e. This double cross-validation is why we adopt its hardware-class stratification and its explicit per-query decomposition as the backbone of the tier table below.

### 2.2 Tiered coefficients by model class

Jegham et al. classify models into five hardware classes by (estimated) parameter count, each assigned a GPU count based on typical serving configurations: Nano (<7B), Micro (7–20B), Small (20–40B), Medium (40–70B), and Large (>70B), assigning 1, 2, 4, or 8 GPUs accordingly [4]. Critically for closed frontier models where parameter counts are undisclosed, models that do not disclose parameter counts, such as OpenAI and Anthropic flagship models (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude-3.7 Sonnet), are classified as Large, OpenAI Mini variants (e.g., GPT-4o mini) as Medium, and models labeled "Nano" as Small based on reported performance (TPS, latency, reasoning capabilities) [4] rather than by disclosed architecture — an unavoidable proxy given closed-model opacity (§6).

We collapse this into three coding-agent-relevant tiers (small/mid/frontier), since a statusline needs a coefficient it can select from a short model-name lookup table without re-deriving a hardware class every release:

| Tier | Represents | e_in (Wh/1K tok) | e_cache (Wh/1K tok) | e_out (Wh/1K tok) | Uncertainty band | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Small** | ≤20B-class models (local/open Small-Nano/Micro tiers; e.g. Haiku-class, GPT-4o-mini-class, Llama-8B-class) | 0.05 | 0.01 | 0.15 | ±2.5x | MODELED — Jegham et al. Nano/Micro hardware-class benchmarks, scaled |
| **Mid** | ~20–70B-class / "Medium" (e.g., Sonnet-class, GPT-4o-mini reasoning variants) | 0.15 | 0.03 | 0.45 | ±2x | MODELED — Jegham et al. Small/Medium classes; consistent with Google's 0.10–0.24 Wh/prompt full-stack range for a mid-size assistant workload |
| **Frontier** | Undisclosed-parameter flagships classified "Large" (e.g., Opus-class, GPT-5-class) and extended-reasoning models | 0.30 | 0.06 | 0.90 | ±3x | MODELED — Jegham et al. Large class (8-GPU); consistent with the 29 Wh/long-prompt ceiling for the most expensive observed configurations at very long context, and with the 18.35 Wh (up to 40 Wh) University of Rhode Island estimate for GPT-5-class queries |

**Accounting boundary of the tier coefficients — an explicit declaration.** Public per-token estimates differ by 2–3x purely on where the measurement boundary sits (Google's own 0.10 accelerator-only vs 0.24 full-stack Wh/prompt, §2.1), so a coefficient table that does not declare its boundary is ambiguous by construction. This table's coefficients are interpreted at the **IT-equipment (node) boundary**: accelerator plus host-node energy, per the Jegham et al. hardware-class methodology (whole-node GPU-count serving configurations). PUE then converts IT energy to facility energy (§1.1). Google's measured `full_stack_scaling_factor` of 1.72x over accelerator-only energy is retained in `coefficients.json` as a **cross-check anchor only** — it already includes facility overhead (Google's fleet PUE ≈ 1.09) as well as host and idle-capacity energy, so multiplying it on top of PUE would double-count facility overhead. The residual ambiguity (whether Jegham-derived node figures fully capture host + provisioned-idle energy the way Google's 1.72x does) is real, is acknowledged here rather than hidden, and is one of the reasons the tier bands are ±2–3x rather than tighter.

**Unknown models.** A model id that matches no lookup entry takes the default (mid) tier's *central* estimate but an envelope spanning the small tier's low bounds to the frontier tier's high bounds — an unrecognized model could plausibly be any of the three classes, and the displayed range must admit that rather than borrowing the mid tier's narrower band. The display additionally carries a visible "?" marker.

**Caching coefficient (e_cache) derivation:** we set `e_cache ≈ 0.2 × e_in` as a first-order approximation. This is not read off a single disclosure; it is inferred from the *pricing* ratio Anthropic and OpenAI publish for cached vs. fresh input tokens (roughly 5–10x cheaper per token for a cache hit — see the attached token-economics reference), on the assumption that provider cache-read pricing tracks the compute actually saved (skipping the attention/KV recomputation over the cached prefix) more closely than it tracks an arbitrary margin decision. This is explicitly labeled **MODELED, price-inferred**, not a physical measurement, and carries a wider uncertainty multiplier (±3x) than the primary in/out coefficients, because the true compute savings from a cache hit depend on prefix length, attention implementation, and whether the "cache" is a KV-cache reuse (near-zero marginal recompute) or merely a discounted-but-still-computed context window.

**Output-token premium:** every source agrees output tokens cost substantially more per token than input tokens, consistent with the autoregressive decode step performing one full forward pass per output token versus prefill's ability to batch the entire prompt through the network in parallel (§3.1). We set `e_out ≈ 3× e_in` across all tiers, matching the ratio implicit in Google's accelerator/host breakdown and in Jegham et al.'s per-query decomposition.

### 2.3 Batch size, hardware generation, and utilization — why these coefficients are not constants

The coefficients above assume the *typical* serving conditions each source measured — they are not physical constants, and stating them without their operating assumptions is itself a source of false precision:

- **Batch size.** Jegham et al. explicitly benchmark under a realistic latency-constrained batch assumption rather than a throughput-maximizing one: AI companies and cloud providers typically rely on dynamic batching to optimize GPU utilization while maintaining low latency; although actual batch sizes fluctuate depending on incoming demand, they are generally constrained to a narrow range below 16 to preserve responsiveness [4], and prior studies show that these latency values are consistent with batch sizes in the range of 4 to 16... accordingly, we adopt a batch size of 8 for all primary calculations [4]. A provider running larger batches (favoring throughput over latency — e.g., for asynchronous/batch-API traffic) would see meaningfully *lower* energy-per-token than these figures, because fixed per-request overhead (KV-cache setup, host-side scheduling) amortizes over more concurrent requests. This is the single largest uncontrolled variable in any outside-in energy estimate and the reason coefficients are given as bands, not points.
- **Hardware generation.** Epoch AI's revision of the ChatGPT energy estimate downward by ~10x was driven substantially by moving the assumed hardware from A100 to H100-class accelerators plus a smaller active-parameter MoE architecture, not by a change in workload. A statusline's coefficient table must be versioned and dated for this reason: the "same" model name served on newer silicon (H100 → B200/GB200-class) can shift the true coefficient by a factor of 2–5x within 12–18 months, independent of anything about the prompt.
- **Utilization.** Epoch AI's 10% typical inference-cluster utilization assumption [1] is doing a lot of work in that estimate: at 100% utilization the same hardware would deliver ~10x more tokens for the same energy draw. Providers do not publish utilization rates, so this is treated as an irreducible uncertainty band contributor (§6), not a value we can pin down.

### 2.4 Where the linear per-token abstraction breaks

The core equation in §1.1 treats energy as strictly linear in token count, with a fixed coefficient per phase (input/cache/output). Four effects break this in practice, and rather than silently absorbing them into the point estimate, we name them and give honest correction guidance:

**Prefill vs. decode asymmetry.** Prefill (processing the prompt) is compute-bound and highly parallelizable — the entire prompt is processed in one or a few forward passes across the full sequence length, achieving high arithmetic intensity and good GPU utilization. Decode (generating output) is memory-bandwidth-bound and inherently serial — one token, one forward pass, one KV-cache read/write cycle at a time, at typically low arithmetic intensity per step. This is precisely why per-output-token energy exceeds per-input-token energy by roughly 3x in every source surveyed (§2.2) rather than being equal, and it means the linear coefficient model is really a *two-regime* model already, not a true single-slope line — the input/output split in §1.1 is the minimum viable correction, not a nicety.

**Batching effects.** Because prefill is compute-bound, doubling batch size roughly doubles compute-per-second but not tokens-per-second-per-request equally; decode's memory-bandwidth bottleneck means batching multiple requests' decode steps together (continuous/dynamic batching) can deliver several-fold more output tokens per joule than serving one request at a time, up to a saturation point set by KV-cache memory capacity. A coefficient measured (implicitly) at batch=8 (§2.3) will *overstate* true marginal energy for a provider running larger batches and *understate* it for a provider running smaller ones — there is no batch-size-invariant per-token coefficient, only a coefficient conditioned on an assumed operating point.

**KV-cache effects.** The KV-cache trades memory for compute: once a prefix's key/value tensors are cached, generating from that prefix again needs no re-computation of attention over it, only reading it from memory. This is the physical mechanism behind cache-hit pricing discounts and is why `e_cache ≪ e_in` (§2.2) — but the magnitude of the saving depends on how much of the sequence is cached versus newly processed, and on whether the cache is resident in fast (HBM/SRAM) or slow (host/disk) memory tiers, which a statusline cannot observe. Long-running coding-agent sessions with large, mostly-static context (a big repository or system prompt reused turn after turn) are the workload where this effect is largest and where treating cached tokens as full-price would most overstate the true footprint.

**Speculative decoding and extended reasoning tokens.** Speculative decoding uses a small draft model to propose multiple tokens which a larger target model verifies in parallel, reducing the number of expensive serial decode steps for a given amount of accepted output — its energy effect is a *discount* relative to naive per-output-token accounting, roughly proportional to the acceptance rate, but with no public per-provider disclosure of acceptance rates this session could source, so we do not attempt a numeric correction and instead flag it as a known downward bias in the "Large/frontier" tier's e_out estimate (SPECULATIVE direction, not magnitude). Extended "thinking"/reasoning tokens (used by o-series/extended-thinking models) run through the *decode* path at full e_out cost even though many are never shown to the user — the statusline's honest response is to count reasoning tokens as regular output tokens in tok_out (since they cost the same energy as visible output), and to display them as a distinguishable sub-total in the token counter so users understand why a "short visible answer" can carry a disproportionate energy line.

## 3. Price-routing-footprint coupling (Task 2)

### 3.1 The claim, stated precisely

The brief for this methodology asks us to argue that inference cost is *partially* coupled to real-time energy markets, and that cost-based routing therefore carries an environmental signal. We evaluate this claim rather than assert it, because the honest answer is: **the causal chain is real in structure but weak and heavily attenuated in practice, and a statusline should not imply routing-by-price is routing-by-carbon.**

The proposed causal chain is:

```
wholesale electricity price (nodal LMP) 
    → data center operating cost 
        → provider serving cost 
            → API sticker price / batch discounts 
                → gateway routing decision 
                    → which facility actually serves a given token
```

Each arrow is a real economic linkage; each arrow is also a lossy one, and the losses compound multiplicatively down the chain.

### 3.2 Link 1: wholesale price → data center operating cost

Electricity markets like ERCOT (Texas) and PJM (mid-Atlantic/Midwest) set **locational marginal prices (LMPs)** that vary by grid node and by hour, reflecting real-time supply/demand balance and transmission congestion. This link is well-established market structure, but its strength for any *specific* data center depends heavily on that facility's procurement contract mix: a data center whose load is substantially covered by a fixed-price Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) is largely insulated from hour-to-hour LMP swings for the PPA-covered portion of its load, while only its residual/marginal load is exposed to spot pricing. Hyperscalers are known to procure significant fractions of their power through long-term PPAs specifically to hedge this exposure — which means the pass-through from "LMP spiked this hour" to "this facility's true marginal cost spiked this hour" is real but structurally damped, and the damping factor is not public per-facility information. **Label: MODELED (market structure), coupling strength weak-to-moderate and facility-dependent.**

### 3.3 Link 2: operating cost → provider serving cost → API price

Even where operating cost does track energy markets, the retail API price a developer sees also embeds capital depreciation (GPU purchase amortization dominates most inference cost structures far more than electricity does at current GPU prices), R&D allocation, margin, and competitive positioning — none of which move with the grid. **The empirical evidence available on this link is that provider pricing does move on hardware-generation and competitive-positioning timescales (months), not grid-price timescales (hours).** No source found in this research links published API sticker prices to hourly wholesale electricity movements. This is the weakest link in the chain and should be labeled accordingly.

**Batch API discounts as revealed preference (the strongest sub-signal we found):** OpenAI Batch API and Anthropic Message Batches API offer roughly a 50% discount for asynchronous processing with a 24-hour turnaround window [17], which is a real, quantified, provider-set price signal that off-peak/deferred/flexible compute is worth roughly half of on-demand compute to the provider. This is the single cleanest piece of "revealed preference" evidence available: it demonstrates providers do price flexibility, which implies flexible load has *some* lower marginal cost to serve (whether from spare off-peak capacity, better batching efficiency, or lower-priced off-peak power). It does not, by itself, prove the saved cost is specifically an *energy-market* saving rather than a *hardware-utilization* saving — those two effects are confounded in a single discount number, and we cannot separate them from public data. **Label: MEASURED (discount exists) + SPECULATIVE (energy-market attribution of the discount's cause).**

**Hyperscaler demand-response programs as a second, more direct sub-signal:** Google's carbon-intelligent computing platform, operating since 2020, shifts compute tasks and their associated energy consumption to times and places where carbon-free energy is available on the grid [9], and was extended in 2023 into an explicit grid demand-response capability: the company developed and piloted a new way to reduce data center electricity consumption when there is high stress on the local power grid, by shifting some non-urgent compute tasks to other times and locations [9]. By 2026, Google had integrated demand response into five utility contracts across the South and Midwest US, making up to 1 gigawatt of its data centers' electricity demand available for curtailment [10] — in exchange, per reporting, for faster grid-interconnection access. This is genuine, documented, operational evidence that **grid conditions do cause real workload/location shifting inside at least one major provider's fleet.** The caveat that matters for a coding-agent statusline: this program is explicitly scoped to **non-urgent/delay-tolerant** compute (described in Google's own materials as batch/training-adjacent workloads), not interactive, latency-sensitive chat/agent inference. There is no public evidence this demand-response/carbon-shifting mechanism is applied to live interactive LLM serving traffic — an agentic coding session is about as latency-sensitive as workloads get. **Label: MEASURED (program exists and is real) + facility/workload-scope caveat is load-bearing (does not obviously extend to interactive inference).**

### 3.4 Link 3: routing decisions on multi-provider gateways

We surveyed OpenRouter's documented routing behavior as the most transparent public example of a production multi-provider gateway (Martian and NotDiamond publish comparatively little public routing-mechanics documentation; LiteLLM is largely a self-hosted proxy library whose routing policy is configured by its operator rather than a fixed platform behavior). OpenRouter's default routing policy is explicit and price-weighted: for each model in a request, OpenRouter's default behavior is to load balance requests across providers, prioritizing price [6], and more specifically by default, OpenRouter sends your request to the cheapest reliable provider, weighted by the inverse square of price [7]. Sort can also be set explicitly to `price`, `latency`, or `throughput`, and percentile-based latency/throughput thresholds can be combined with price sorting so that cost is minimized subject to a performance floor.

Two findings bound how much environmental signal this carries:

1. **Routing is price-driven by default, and OpenRouter states explicitly that it does not mark up provider pricing** — pricing shown in the model catalog is what you pay, which is exactly what you will see on the provider's own website [8]. This means the price the router optimizes against is the provider's *retail* price, which (per §3.3) is only weakly and slowly coupled to real-time energy costs. The router is therefore optimizing a signal that is itself several links removed from grid conditions.
2. **No public gateway (OpenRouter, and by extension the less-documented Martian/NotDiamond) currently routes on a carbon or energy signal at all.** Routing criteria documented are exclusively price, latency/throughput percentiles, uptime/outage history, tool-call support, context-length support, and (for the NotDiamond-powered Auto Router) prompt-to-model capability matching. There is no observed "route to the lowest-carbon provider" feature in the current gateway landscape.

### 3.5 Overall coupling-strength assessment

| Link | Coupling strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Wholesale LMP → facility operating cost | Weak–moderate, facility-dependent | MODELED — attenuated by PPA/fixed-price procurement share, which is not public per facility |
| Operating cost → API sticker price | Very weak on hourly timescales; present on multi-month timescales (hardware refresh, competition) | MODELED — no evidence found of intraday price moves tracking grid prices |
| Batch/off-peak discounts as flexibility signal | Moderate (discount is measured; energy-specific attribution is not) | MEASURED discount + SPECULATIVE causal attribution |
| Hyperscaler demand response ↔ grid carbon signal | Real and documented, but scoped to non-urgent/batch workloads | MEASURED, with an explicit scope caveat for interactive inference |
| Gateway price-based routing → environmental outcome | Present only insofar as routing tracks price, and price only weakly tracks grid conditions (compounds the two weak links above) | MODELED, net effect judged weak |

**Conclusion for the statusline's design:** cost-based routing carries a real but faint and currently *unmeasurable-from-outside* environmental signal. The methodology therefore does **not** attempt to infer a per-request carbon adjustment from which provider/route was used, or to claim that a "cheaper" route was a "greener" route. Where a future gateway begins exposing routing telemetry that names the actual serving facility or region (see roadmap), that is the point at which this coupling becomes directly actionable rather than theoretical — until then, the honest statusline design uses the coefficient/region-preset approach (§2, §4) keyed on the *model* and a *user-declared or provider-disclosed region*, not on an inferred price-to-carbon relationship.

## 4. Temporal water theory (Task 3)

### 4.1 Why water intensity is time-dependent, formally

Water intensity in the core model (§1.2) is a sum of two independently time-varying terms, following the decomposition in Ren et al.: on-site WUE, denoted rho_s1,t, is defined as the ratio of the on-site water consumption to server energy consumption and varies over time depending on the outside temperature [5], and off-site scope-2 WUE, denoted rho_s2,t, is the ratio of off-site water consumption for each kWh of electricity consumption and measures the electricity water intensity factor (EWIF) [5].

**On-site term (WUE_site).** rho_s1,t increases significantly for cooling towers when the outside wet-bulb temperature increases, and increases for outside-air cooling when the outside dry-bulb temperature is too hot or the humidity is too low [5]. Physically: evaporative/adiabatic cooling systems (cooling towers, direct/indirect evaporative cooling) work by evaporating water into an airstream to reject heat, and their water consumption per unit of heat rejected rises as the ambient wet-bulb temperature climbs, because the thermodynamic margin between the wet-bulb temperature and the target cooling-water temperature narrows — more water must evaporate to achieve the same heat rejection. This creates three nested time-dependencies a full model would need: **diurnal** (wet-bulb typically lower overnight than mid-afternoon), **seasonal** (summer vs. winter wet-bulb swings of 15–25°C in continental climates), and **economizer-hours** (many modern designs use free/outside-air cooling with no water at all below a wet-bulb or dry-bulb threshold, and only activate evaporative assist above it — meaning WUE_site can be genuinely ~0 for large fractions of the year in cool/dry climates and rise sharply only during hot-humid windows). Microsoft's own fleet commentary corroborates the economizer-threshold mechanism directly: by 2008, Microsoft shifted towards direct air cooling with evaporative assist across its fleet — this approach uses outside air and introduces water only when temperatures exceed 29.4°C (85°F), cutting water consumption by up to 90% compared to traditional systems [15].

**The live-signal implementation of WUE_site is a two-signal model.** The `modelfootprint` live layer implements the mechanism above as: (1) a **dry-bulb economizer gate** — direct-air-with-evaporative-assist fleets (the Microsoft-disclosed architecture) introduce water only as ambient air temperature approaches the disclosed 29.4°C/85°F threshold, so engagement ramps linearly over the ~6°C below it; and (2) a **wet-bulb draw-intensity factor** — within the engaged band, water consumed per unit heat rejected rises with wet-bulb temperature (the approach margin narrows), scaling the draw between a floor fraction and 1.0 across a ~5–25°C wet-bulb range. The result is always bounded by the region preset's low/high band and labeled MODELED: facility-level thresholds and response curves are not public, and a dry hot day (30°C/10% RH, wet-bulb ≈15°C) genuinely draws less water per kWh of heat rejected than a humid one (30°C/90% RH, wet-bulb ≈29°C) under this model — the humidity dimension a dry-bulb-only ramp would miss.

**Off-site term (EWIF_grid).** a common estimation method is weighted averaging: rho_s2,t = sum_k(b_k,t × EWIF_k) / sum_k(b_k,t), where b_k,t denotes the amount of electricity generated from fuel type k at time t for the grid serving the data center, and EWIF_k is the water intensity factor for fuel type k [5]. Thermoelectric generation (coal, nuclear, and most natural-gas steam-cycle plants) consumes substantial cooling water per MWh; combustion-turbine gas peakers, wind, and solar PV consume comparatively little. Because grid operators dispatch different generation mixes at different hours (baseload thermal overnight, gas peakers and renewables shifting the mix during the day, renewable curtailment/output varying with sun and wind), **the marginal generation mix serving a new increment of load at 3pm can have a very different EWIF than the mix serving it at 3am**, independent of anything about the on-site cooling system. This is a separate time-dependency from WUE_site and must not be conflated with it — a facility with excellent (near-zero) on-site WUE can still carry a large total water footprint if its grid's marginal generation mix is water-intensive at the hours it draws power, and vice versa.

### 4.2 Withdrawal vs. consumption — a distinction that must survive the whole pipeline

Water withdrawal refers to freshwater taken from the ground or surface water sources, either temporarily or permanently, and then used for agricultural, industrial, or municipal uses [5], indicating "the level of competition as well as dependence on water resources," while water consumption is defined as water withdrawal minus water returned [5] to the source. Withdrawal overstates local impact for systems that return most water (e.g., once-through cooling that discharges warmed water back to a river); consumption better represents permanent removal from the local water balance (evaporated water, water embedded in a product, water discharged to a different watershed or as vapor).

Google explicitly adopts the consumption framing for its own headline number: water consumption efficiency is measured using the Water Usage Effectiveness metric, specifically the consumptive-use variant (ISO WUE Category 2), calculated as water input minus water returned; though both withdrawal and consumption intensity are calculated, consumption is considered to best represent impact on local water availability, as it represents the volume of water evaporated and therefore unavailable for reuse [2], further noting Google consumes approximately 80% of the water it withdraws, on average [2]. Microsoft's public WUE figures (§4.3) are also consumption-based by their stated formula (water used for humidification and cooling ÷ IT energy), not raw withdrawal.

**Design rule: the statusline must label every water figure as consumption or withdrawal, never leave it ambiguous, and should default to consumption** (matching both major public disclosures used as sources here) since consumption is the more locally meaningful and more consistently disclosed basis.

### 4.3 Region preset table

Public, named, region-level WUE disclosures are rare — most operators publish only a fleet-wide average. The clearest fleet-wide figure available is Microsoft's FY25 (July 2024–June 2025) disclosure:

Microsoft's average WUE decreased from 2.3 L/kWh in the early 2000s to 0.27 L/kWh in 2025 [11], a **MEASURED, self-reported** fleet-wide average (owned-and-controlled datacenters operational 12+ months), calculated as annual liters of water used for humidification and cooling, divided by total annual kWh used to power IT equipment [12]. For comparison, the industry average WUE is cited at 0.84 L/kWh, with Amazon claiming an industry-leading rating of 0.12 L/kWh [13]. Regionally, Microsoft states its Phoenix, Arizona datacenters achieved a 23% year-over-year WUE improvement in FY25 alone [11] through direct-evaporative-cooling optimization, and separately that approximately 90 percent of its 2025-owned fleet now operates on low- to zero-water cooling systems, with water required less than five percent of the time in cooler regions such as Dublin and Amsterdam, and up to 40 percent [of the time] in hotter regions [14] — establishing the qualitative shape of the region spread (near-continuous free cooling in cool/temperate Northern Europe vs. substantial active-cooling hours in hot arid/humid climates) even though Microsoft's FY25 report does not appear to publish a single clean per-region WUE table in liters/kWh that we could independently verify.

**A specific numeric claim requires an explicit reliability flag here.** A third-party industry blog (Introl, not a primary Microsoft disclosure) states a Microsoft Arizona WUE of 1.52 L/kWh against a Singapore figure of 0.02 L/kWh — figures we could **not** corroborate against Microsoft's own published tables in this research pass. We do not include this pairing as a coefficient-table entry; it is flagged here only to be transparent about a claim we found circulating but chose not to adopt without primary-source confirmation. The brief's own reference figures (Phoenix ≈ 1.0 L/kWh, Dublin ≈ 0.01 L/kWh, ≈75x spread) are of the same order and directionally consistent with everything corroborated above (hot-arid >> cool-humid, and a several-tens-of-times spread is entirely plausible given the economizer-threshold mechanism in §4.1), but should likewise be treated as **directional / order-of-magnitude**, not as an audited Microsoft-published number, pending a live disclosure feed (see roadmap).

## 5. Uncertainty propagation and display precision (Task 4)

### 5.1 Every coefficient carries a central estimate and a range, never a bare point

All coefficients in `coefficients.json` are stored as `{central, low, high, label, source}` tuples, never as bare scalars. The statusline computes a point estimate for the running total (for readability) but should expose the range on hover/expand, and the range is not cosmetic — it is the propagated product of the per-stage uncertainty multipliers documented in §2–§4:

- Energy: tier coefficient uncertainty (±2–3x, §2.2) is the dominant term. PUE uncertainty is comparatively small (well-disclosed, typically 1.1–1.3 for modern hyperscale facilities) and is not a major amplifier.
- Water: energy uncertainty carries through multiplicatively, compounded by WUE_site regional-proxy uncertainty (§4.3, itself a ±2–3x band within a climate class) and by EWIF's genuine hour-to-hour variability (which can easily span 2x within a single day on a mixed-generation grid). Compounding two independent ±2–3x factors multiplicatively can widen the total water-estimate band to roughly an order of magnitude in the worst case (a hot-arid facility during a peak-demand, coal-heavy evening vs. a mild afternoon on the same grid) — this is not a bug in the propagation, it is an honest reflection of how much less controlled the water estimate is than the energy estimate underneath it.
- Carbon: inherits energy uncertainty, compounded by the location-based vs. market-based choice (§1.4, a further ~3.7x swing by definition, not by error) and by grid-mix hourly variability similar in kind to EWIF's.

**Practical combination rule used in `coefficients.json`, and what it is honestly called:** the displayed [low–high] figures are computed by taking every factor at its low bound simultaneously (for the low) and every factor at its high bound simultaneously (for the high). This is a **scenario envelope** — "if everything broke pessimistic/optimistic at once" — and implementers, docs, and display text must call it that, not a confidence interval or an independent-error propagation. Selecting all extremes at once ignores both the correlations between factors and the improbability of simultaneous extremes, so the envelope is wider than a formal (e.g., root-sum-square-in-log-space or Monte Carlo) combination would produce; that conservatism is preferred here precisely because the underlying inputs do not support distributional claims. A formal propagation with declared distributions and correlation groups is a legitimate future upgrade — but only when the inputs earn it.

**Carbon-specific envelope terms:** the static location-based fallback (a Google-fleet proxy of 345 gCO2e/kWh, not the user's actual region) carries a 150–600 gCO2e/kWh envelope in `coefficients.json` reflecting the plausible spread of average CI across major datacenter grids and hours; a live grid CI reading replaces it with a MODELED ±25% band for forecast/measurement error. Both are labeled in the data; neither converts the envelope into a confidence interval.

### 5.2 Recommended display precision

Given the bands above, we recommend:

- **Energy:** display to 2 significant figures (e.g., "0.31 Wh", not "0.3142857 Wh"). A ±2–3x band around a base estimate means the third significant figure is meaningless.
- **Water:** display to 1–2 significant figures and prefer a qualitative range indicator (e.g., a small ± glyph or a "~" prefix) over a bare number, given the wider compounded band in §5.1.
- **Carbon (if shown):** always paired with an explicit "(location-based)" or "(market-based)" tag in the same visual unit as the number — never a bare gCO2e figure — because the accounting-basis choice alone is a multi-x swing (§1.4) that a user cannot infer from the number.
- **Never display more precision than the coefficient's own stated uncertainty band supports.** This is the single most important anti-greenwashing design constraint in this methodology: a tool that shows "14.2837 Wh" invites exactly the scrutiny this document is trying to survive, because it visually claims a precision no input data can support.

### 5.3 What is fundamentally unknowable from outside a provider, and how the methodology stays honest about it

A statusline running on a developer's machine, calling a commercial provider's API, cannot observe:

- **Actual hardware serving the request.** Providers do not disclose per-request or even per-model accelerator type/generation; the tier table (§2.2) is a proxy built on inferred hardware classes (§2.2, Jegham et al.'s classification method), not ground truth.
- **Actual batch occupancy at request time.** The single largest lever on true marginal energy-per-token (§2.3) is invisible from outside; providers do not expose real-time batch size or queue occupancy.
- **Which physical datacenter/region actually served a given API call.** Even where a provider names a "region" (e.g., a cloud region selector), the mapping from that logical region to a specific facility, and that facility's specific PUE/WUE/local grid mix at that hour, is provider-internal information.
- **Whether a provider's stated regional infrastructure is currently under a demand-response curtailment event, a maintenance-driven failover, or any other transient condition affecting its true footprint that hour.**

The methodology's response to this irreducible opacity is structural, not evasive: (1) every displayed number is explicitly tiered/bucketed rather than presented as request-specific ground truth; (2) every number carries its uncertainty band in the underlying data structure even when display compresses it; (3) the roadmap (§7 / `LIVE_SIGNAL_ROADMAP.md`) is designed around *provider-side transparency improvements and third-party grid/weather APIs*, not around trying to reverse-engineer provider internals; and (4) the FAQ (`LIMITATIONS_AND_FAQ.md`) states this limitation to the user in plain language rather than letting a confident-looking number imply certainty it doesn't have.
